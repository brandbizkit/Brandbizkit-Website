Picture two businesses on the same street. One is a 60-person restaurant group with a manager who lives in spreadsheets. The other is a seven-person café where the owner still does payroll at the kitchen table on Sunday nights.

Guess which one is using AI to get its weekends back.

New data says it's almost always the bigger one. That gap is the most important AI story for small business owners right now.

## The adoption number hides a split

Homebase's 2026 Main Street AI Gap Report, a survey of 750 U.S. decision-makers at businesses with 5–499 employees, puts overall AI adoption at 74%. That's up from 64% a year earlier. Sounds like the whole of Main Street is on board.

It isn't. Break the number down by size and it falls apart.

[chart:0]

Among businesses earning under $500K a year, only 54% use or are piloting AI. At $5M and up, it's 81%. That's a 27-point gap. Team size shows the same split: 57% of 5–9 person teams versus 84% of teams with 50–99 people.

So the businesses with the least time to spare are the ones getting the least help from AI.

## And it's not because AI isn't working

You might assume the smallest owners looked at AI, tried it, and decided it was hype. The data says the opposite.

In Homebase's survey, 89% of AI adopters reported a positive impact on their business. Only 0.4% reported a negative one. Businesses using AI for both scheduling and payroll saved about 7.7 hours and $343 a month, roughly $4,100 a year.

For a seven-person café, that's not a rounding error. That's a full workday every month, plus a real chunk of margin.

## It's not lack of interest, either

Here's the part that should change how you think about this. Among small businesses not using AI yet, 62% say they're still interested.

So what's actually stopping them?

[chart:1]

Privacy concerns lead at 38%, then fear of AI making mistakes (31%) and plain distrust (27%). And 18% simply don't know which tools exist.

A Zensurance survey released in September 2026, covering 1,000 Canadian small business owners and self-employed professionals points the same way. 32% said AI has had little to no impact on their business. Another 27% said they aren't sure how it even applies to what they do. Just 9% credited AI with meaningful productivity gains or cost savings.

Put those together and the barrier looks a lot less like money and a lot more like clarity. Most of the AI advice out there is written for companies with an IT person, a data team and time to experiment. A solo owner reads it and reasonably concludes, "this isn't for me."

## Why the gap will widen if you wait

Bigger businesses aren't just ahead. They're compounding. Every hour AI saves on admin is an hour back on sales, service or growth. Homebase found 57% of AI users put their saved time toward growing the business.

There's also a cost to staying manual. 46% of respondents said manual errors cost them $500 or more a month. Scheduling mix-ups, payroll mistakes and inventory miscounts are exactly the boring jobs AI handles well now.

The longer a small business waits, the more it pays for that friction while competitors stop paying it.

## How a small team closes the gap

You don't need an AI strategy deck. You need one job.

- **Pick your most annoying repeat task.** Scheduling, payroll prep, inventory counts or customer replies. The adopters in Homebase's data started with exactly these jobs: data analysis (47%), payroll and timekeeping (45%) and inventory (44%).
- **Use tools built into software you already pay for.** Many scheduling, accounting and POS platforms now include AI features. That sidesteps much of the privacy worry, because your data stays where it already lives.
- **Keep a human check for the first month.** Fear of mistakes is a fair concern. Review the output until the tool earns your trust.
- **Measure in hours.** Write down how long the task took before, then after. If it saves you a Sunday night a month, keep going.

## The takeaway

AI is working for small businesses, and the data is clear on that. What isn't working is the way it gets explained to the smallest ones. The 27-point gap between a sub-$500K shop and a $5M company isn't about ambition or budget. It's about someone showing you where AI fits in a business your size.
